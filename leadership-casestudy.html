Introduction.

A designer who asks for a promotion and gets warm encouragement usually leaves within a year. A designer who gets a plan stays, because they can see the next step and who is judging it.


At Matillion I managed three designers and took one of them from junior to mid level. I did it by making the criteria visible and measuring against them over time. Alongside that I worked with the Director of Product on how the company levels designers and how it hires them.

Why this mattered.

A promotion that rests on a manager saying trust me fails at the first hard question. The people deciding sit above the manager. They have not watched the work week by week. They need the case laid out against criteria they already recognise.

It also matters to the designer. Without a method they are left guessing whether they are one project away or a year away. Guessing is how good people quietly start looking elsewhere.

The call I made.

I treated the request as a design problem and not a favour. The designer raised it in our weekly one to one. I could have encouraged them and promised to raise it later. Instead I took the company levelling framework as the brief and set out to close the gap between where they were and what the next level asked for.

How I made it real.

I started by letting them describe their own gaps. We ran a mentoring workshop on a shared board. They marked the skills they wanted to develop and the work they loved and hated. They placed themselves on a range for each skill and drew an arrow for which way they wanted to move. We also wrote down what each of us was committing to.

I then mapped those gaps against the levelling framework. We agreed six areas to prepare for promotion and I turned them into a mentoring plan. The plan carried continuous assessment, so we checked progress against the framework as we went and not at the end.

When the evidence was there I helped write the promotion proposal. It went to the VP of Product and the CPO.

Where it landed.

The proposal met no pushback from senior leadership. The designer became more confident in their work and presented it more often. The quality of the work improved. They explored problems more deeply and generated more ideas before settling.

Beyond my own team.

I worked with the Director of Product to define levelling and the hiring profile for a principal designer role. I then helped hire two principal designers, a staff designer and a junior designer. Only the junior joined my team. The others were placed elsewhere in the organisation, so the hiring shaped design well beyond the people who reported to me.

Reflection.

What I would keep is the order. Let the person describe the gaps first, then measure against the criteria the company already uses, then write the case. What I would change is capturing the evidence as I went. The plan tracked progress, but I did not keep a record that would let me show you the before and after.

The principal move was making the criteria visible to the person being judged and to the people judging them.
